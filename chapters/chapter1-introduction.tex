\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chapter:introduction}

\section{Background}

Traditionally, hiring used to run on paper. Job seekers printed resumes and delivered them in person or by post, and employers advertised openings in newspapers and reviewed applications by hand.\\
With the rapid growth of Internet technologies, the recruitment process has transformed as online recruitment platforms appear: employers now publish vacancies and candidates apply electronically. This digital transformation has reduced geographical barriers and accelerated communication between employers and candidates.

The scale at which this transformation now operates is considerable. Vietnam's labor force numbered approximately 52.9 million people in 2024 \cite{gso2024lfs}, and platforms built to match workers to jobs serve a comparable share of it: TopCV alone reports over 8 million candidate profiles and more than 540,000 registered employers \cite{topcv2023report}. Yet scale has not resolved the underlying friction. A nationwide survey of 996 Vietnamese professionals found that 35\% were actively seeking a new job at the time of the survey, with a further 21\% planning to start within six months, and workers reported becoming still more likely to job-hunt when economic conditions worsen \cite{michaelpage2023}. Against this volume of competing job seekers, hiring itself remains slow: a global survey of recruiters found that time-to-hire averages 44 days from an opening being posted to an offer being made \cite{amsbersin2023}, longer still for specialized roles. It is this combination - a large, actively searching workforce and a slow, largely manual matching process - that a centralized recruitment platform is positioned to address.

\section{Problem Statement}

The new approach brings its own friction. Each recruitment site has its own search interface, its own resume format, and its own application flow. A candidate who wants to reach more than one pool of jobs must recreate essentially the same profile and resume on every platform, then visit each one separately to check whether an application has progressed. The more platforms a job seeker uses, the more duplicated effort the search costs.\\
Employers face similar challenge in reverse. Publishing a posting is only the first step; they still have to collect applications, read resumes, record decisions, and keep those records straight. Without a single place that ties postings, applications, and decisions together, the employer ends up stitching information across tools, recreating the inefficiency the platform was supposed to remove.

\section{Aim, Objectives and Scope}

The thesis aims to analyze the design and implementation of such centralized system: a platform where job seekers search jobs, maintain a profile, upload a resume, apply, and track their applications, and where employers manage company information, publish postings, and review applicants through one interface.\\
The implemented system consist of several functional modules. This thesis will discuss two modules as its primary focus. The supporting modules are built and described, but only briefly.

\section{Methodology}

Development was driven by use case documentation rather than a single fixed process. Work was decomposed into use case groups - profile, resume, job application, employer, and so on - and each group passed through the same small loop. First the use cases of the group were documented with their specifications and diagrams. Then the grouping was implemented, backend first and frontend after. Wherever implementation surfaced a use case that had not been anticipated in the documentation, the loop ran again: the new use case was implemented if it was needed immediately, and was always written back into the documentation so that the analysis in Chapter \ref{chapter:system-analysis} and the implementation in Chapter \ref{chapter:implementation} would stay consistent with each other.\\

\section{Thesis Organization}

The remainder of the thesis follows the loop above. Chapter \ref{chapter:system-analysis} analyzes the requirements: actors, the functional and non-functional requirements, and the use cases of the featured modules. Chapter \ref{chapter:system-design} turns those requirements into design, covering the system architecture, the database schema, and the module-level design. Chapter \ref{chapter:implementation} describes how the design was implemented, and Chapter \ref{chapter:testing} summarizes verification. Chapter \ref{chapter:conclusion} closes with the results and limitations of the project.\\
